Methods & sources

How the comparison works

  1. Your reading is converted to Newtons and divided by your body weight (in Newtons) to give force as % of body weight. Normalising to body weight removes most of the size effect, so heavier and lighter people can be judged on the same scale.
  2. The value is compared with the mean and standard deviation (SD) for healthy people of your sex, age decade and limb side (dominant / non-dominant) from the reference study.
  3. A z-score (how many SDs you are from the mean) and the corresponding percentile are shown. Following Andrews & Bohannon, a value more than 2 SD below the mean is flagged as weak; −1 to −2 SD as below average.
  4. Where the source paper published a regression equation on sex, age and body weight, your reading is also shown as a % of the force predicted for someone exactly like you.

Test positions used by the reference studies

Hand-held dynamometer norms are only valid if you test the same way. Most actions were gravity-neutralised make tests: build to maximal effort over ~2 s and hold ~5 s while the tester keeps the dynamometer still. Best of two trials. Knee-flexion norms for ages 20–49 come from Douma et al., who used a seated break test (tester overpowers the subject).

Caveats

  • Reference samples were convenience samples of healthy volunteers; values differ between countries, devices and testers. Treat ratings as a guide, not a diagnosis.
  • Knee-extension norms for younger men are under-estimated because many subjects exceeded the dynamometer ceiling (650 N in 1997; 512 N in 1996).
  • Plantarflexion and knee extension are hard for a tester to resist by hand; readings may reflect tester strength as much as yours.
  • Bohannon 1997 did not test knee flexion. Ages 20–49 use Douma 2014 (break test, % BW from group mean weight). Ages 50–79 use Andrews 1996 (make test, % BW). There is still no published HHD % BW table for hamstrings in children or ages 80+.
  • For ages 80+ the 70–79 decade is used and marked extrapolated. For children and adolescents only the 1000 Norms Project (absolute force) is available, and that project measured knee flexors with fixed dynamometry (Nm), not HHD.
  • The 1000 Norms Project tested the dominant limb only; its values are applied to both sides here.
  • This tool is for education and self-tracking. It is not medical advice.
